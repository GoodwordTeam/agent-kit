# The strategy interview

Read at the start of the interview, and again for each section an update run revisits. Every section
here maps to one section of the strategy template.

For each section: ask the opening question, judge the answer against the bar, push back when it falls
into one of the named failures, and capture the final answer in the human's own words.

## Rules for every section

1. **Ask, don't prescribe.** Free-form answers for purpose, positioning and users. Offer choices only
   for routing, such as which section to revisit.
2. **Push back once, maybe twice.** Name the specific problem and ask a sharper question. After the
   second weak answer, capture what was given and mark the section worth revisiting.
3. **Quote the human back.** Challenge with their exact words. A paraphrase softens the challenge.
4. **One to three sentences per answer.** If a paragraph comes back, ask which sentence matters most.
5. **Keep the failure names to yourself.** Ask the sharper question; do not announce "that's a vanity
   metric".
6. **Ground the question, not the answer.** Open with what the grounding suggests ("the README reads
   like X; is that the problem?") and let the human confirm or correct. Where stated intent and the
   answer disagree, that disagreement is the pushback, and the answer is what gets captured.

## 1. Purpose

**Ask:** "What's the core problem this product solves, and what makes that problem hard?"

A strong answer names a specific situation the user is in and the crux that makes it hard now, and is
falsifiable. Push back on:

- A goal stated as a problem ("we need to grow revenue"): what in the world makes that goal hard?
- A vague wish ("people need better tools"): whose situation, doing what, trying what today?
- A symptom ("users churn after 30 days"): what underlying condition produces it?
- A problem too broad to affect: narrow it to users, a task and the moment it hurts.
- A missing feature: what outcome would the feature give them?

**Capture:** one or two sentences, the situation and the crux. No solution language.

## 2. Positioning

**Ask:** "Given that problem, what's your approach: the commitment that makes it tractable?"

A strong answer is a choice that implies alternatives not pursued, general enough to direct many
decisions and specific enough to rule some out. Push back on values ("we move fast"), a feature list,
a description of what the product does, the goal restated, several approaches at once, an approach
with no line back to the purpose, and a claim the nearest competitor could truthfully make too.

**Capture:** one or two sentences, ideally implying "so that" and the purpose's outcome.

## 3. Users

**Ask:** "Who is the primary user, and what job are they hiring this product to do?"

A strong answer names one primary persona by role or situation and states the job as a verb phrase.
Push back on several primaries at once, demographics, a role with no situation, and a generic job
("be more productive").

**Capture:** a persona name and one job sentence. Secondary personas only if truly necessary.

## 4. Key metrics

**Ask:** "What three to five metrics will tell you whether the approach is working?"

A strong answer stays at three to five, mixes something that moves weekly with something that moves
quarterly, and could regress if the product got worse. Push back on counts that only go up, totals
that rise while the product worsens, team outputs instead of user outcomes, a dozen metrics, and
anything with no way to check it on an ordinary Tuesday.

**Capture:** each metric with a one-line definition and where it is measured, if known. If it is
measured nowhere, ask whether it can start being measured. Record where a metric lives, never what it
reads today.

## 5. Tracks

**Ask:** "What are the two to four tracks of work you're investing in to carry out the approach?"

A track is a domain of investment that follows from the positioning, broad enough to hold several
features. Push back on features in disguise, seven tracks, one track, a track with no link to the
approach, and "improve the product".

Recent commits help here, and only here: offer them as a question ("most recent work is in X; is that
a track, a temporary push, or unrelated?"). A burst of work does not make a track.

**Capture:** two to four tracks, each with a name, a one-line purpose and why it serves the approach.

## Stress test

Run it once sections 1 to 5 are captured, before Boundaries. Pose three to five concrete proposals,
one at a time, aimed at the draft's fault lines: a tempting feature just off the approach, a second
persona pulling another way, a track that would starve another, a metric that would look good while
the approach failed. Choose proposals the human could reasonably answer either way; skip foregone
conclusions.

- The draft already decides it the same way: confirmed, move on.
- The draft cannot decide it, or decides it differently: the positioning or a track is too vague; ask
  the sharpening question and update that answer.
- The human resists the proposal: a candidate for Boundaries.

One round per proposal. Never add a section or a metric to absorb a proposal.

## 6. Boundaries (always written)

**Ask:** "Is there anything you've decided not to do right now that's worth naming? Things the team
keeps being tempted by."

Offer the proposals the human resisted; invent none. One sentence each. From the resisted proposals,
draft one line completing "Resist a change when ...". When nothing came up, the section says so in one
line, so readers always have a place to look.

## 7. Milestones (optional)

**Ask:** "Any dated milestones worth anchoring: a launch, a fundraise, a renewal? Skip if none."

Externally visible dates only, captured verbatim. Default is to skip; never turn it into a schedule.

## 8. Brand (optional)

**Ask:** "Any one-liner or key message you want the page to carry? Skip if not yet."

Two or three lines at most.

## Why these questions

Purpose, Positioning and Tracks follow the kernel in Richard Rumelt's *Good Strategy Bad Strategy*:
diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action. The pushback targets what he calls bad strategy:
fluff, goals dressed as strategy, and feature lists in place of a choice.
